Requestor Guide

Requestor Guide

This page is for anyone using an Bracken-powered application — voting in a private DAO, joining a confidential auction, contributing data to a private analytics round, or anything else built on top of Encrypted Execution Environments (E3s).

If you're a developer integrating with the Bracken contracts directly, see Building with Bracken and the SDK reference. The page you're reading now is the plain-English version: what you're paying for, what to do when something goes wrong, and how to read the security knobs on a request.

Most of what an E3 does is invisible by design — your inputs are encrypted before they ever leave your device, and only the result is revealed. This guide explains the few things that are visible to you: the fee, the waiting period, and what happens if a round fails.

1. What you're paying for

Every E3 request charges a one-time fee, paid in the protocol's configured fee token. The Ethereum Mainnet launch uses USDS. The fee covers four things, in roughly this order of cost:

  1. Selecting a Ciphernode committee — a random subset of operators who will hold a share of the decryption key for your round.
  2. Generating the shared encryption key (DKG). This is the cryptographically expensive step and is the largest line item on your bill.
  3. Running the actual computation through the compute provider.
  4. Decrypting the result and posting it on-chain.

Pricing is cost-plus: the protocol models the ciphernode work, applies a configurable margin, then splits the final E3 fee between the protocol treasury and the operators who actually did work. Launch pricing uses a 10% margin and a 1.82% treasury share of the gross E3 fee. That 1.82% gross share is approximately 20% of the margin; ticket purchases are operator capacity deposits, not normal protocol revenue.

The fee is charged once, when you submit the request, and held in escrow until the round finishes. You don't get a refund on success — that's the price of the computation. You do get a refund (full or partial, depending on when it failed) on failure.

Mainnet deployments can keep E3 requests paused during operator onboarding. If requests are paused, request() reverts until governance enables them.

2. The lifecycle of a request

From the moment you submit until the result is published, every E3 walks through five phases:

#PhaseWhat happensWhat you see
1RequestYour fee is locked; a committee is chosen by random sortition.A "request submitted" confirmation.
2Key generationThe committee runs a distributed key-generation ceremony and publishes a public key.Usually 5–20 minutes, depending on size.
3Input windowYou (and possibly other participants) encrypt your inputs to that key and submit them.Whatever input form the dApp shows.
4ComputeThe compute provider runs the program over the encrypted inputs and posts an encrypted result.A waiting state — nothing for you to do.
5Decrypt & publishThe committee jointly decrypts the result and publishes it on-chain.The plaintext result appears in the dApp.

The application you're using will surface phase changes for you. If you want to inspect them yourself, the Bracken contract emits an E3StageChanged event for every transition.

3. When things go wrong

A round can fail for a handful of reasons. Each one matches a specific phase, and each one comes with a different refund split. The general rule is simple:

You get refunded for the work that wasn't completed. Honest operators get paid for the work that was completed. A configurable failure-path protocol share (default 5%) covers overhead.

Concretely, every E3 that ends in a failed state is tagged with a FailureReason:

Failure reasonWhen it happensRoughly what you get back
CommitteeFormationTimeoutNot enough operators showed up to form a committee in time.~95%
InsufficientCommitteeMembersThe registry couldn't supply enough eligible operators.~95%
DKGTimeoutThe committee never finished generating the encryption key.~85%
DKGInvalidSharesAn operator submitted bad key shares and was caught.~85%
NoInputsReceivedNobody submitted any inputs during the input window.~55%
ComputeTimeoutThe compute provider didn't post a result before its deadline.~55%
ComputeProviderExpiredThe compute provider signalled it couldn't finish.~55%
ComputeProviderFailedThe compute provider returned a verifiable error.~55%
RequesterCancelledYou cancelled the E3 before it completed.The unspent work allocation
DecryptionTimeoutThe committee never published the decryption.~0% (work was already done)
DecryptionInvalidSharesAn operator submitted bad decryption shares and was caught.~0% — but slashed bonds top up your refund
VerificationFailedThe decryption proof didn't verify.~0%

Percentages are based on the default refund policy. Governance can re-tune them; the live values are readable from E3RefundManager.getWorkAllocation() if you want to verify before requesting.

A useful mental model: the further along a round got before failing, the more of your fee was already spent on legitimate work. Failures during decryption are the worst case for the requester's refund, but they're also the case where misbehaving operators get slashed — and those slashed bonds are added back to your refund automatically. In some failed rounds you can end up receiving more than your initial fee.

How a failed round actually gets marked failed

Most failures are detected automatically by the protocol's slashing manager and mark the round failed in the same transaction. A few timeout-based failures (the *Timeout reasons in the table) need someone to push the round across the finish line by calling markE3Failed(e3Id) on the Bracken contract. This call is permissionless — the application you're using will usually do it for you, but you can do it yourself from a block explorer if it's stalled.

Cancelling your request

The address that submitted an E3 can call cancelE3(e3Id) while the E3 is active. Cancellation is final. The protocol records the current stage, pays operators only for milestones that finished before the cancellation, and returns the remaining work allocation to you. With the default policy, you receive about 95% before committee finalization, 85% after committee finalization, and 55% after the public key is published. The protocol share is retained in each case.

Cancellation marks the E3 as failed with RequesterCancelled. Refund calculation is a separate, permissionless step so a temporary registry or token failure cannot prevent the cancellation itself. After processE3Failure(e3Id) succeeds, claim the refund through the normal flow below.

Claiming your refund

Refunds are pull-based: once a round is marked failed and the distribution is calculated, you have to claim. The flow is:

  1. Confirm the round is in Failed state. The application will usually show this; you can also check by reading getE3Stage(e3Id).
  2. Wait for the RefundDistributionCalculated event (it's emitted as soon as the slashing manager finalises the round — usually the same transaction that marked it failed).
  3. Call claimRequesterRefund(e3Id) on the E3RefundManager contract from the same address that originally submitted the request. The refund is paid in the same token your original fee was paid in, even if governance has rotated the fee token in the meantime.

Most dApps will offer a "claim refund" button that does this for you. If you submitted from a smart-contract wallet (e.g. Safe), use the same wallet to claim.

There's no deadline for claiming. Your refund sits in the refund manager indefinitely until you take it.

4. Things to know before you submit

A few practical points that catch people out:

  • Approve only the exact fee. Use the dApp's "estimate" / getE3Quote flow to read the exact fee, and approve that amount. Don't grant unlimited approvals to the Bracken contract.
  • You can't top up later. If the protocol's pricing changes after you submit, your locked fee is what runs your round. Don't try to amend an in-flight request — submit a new one if needed.
  • Set realistic windows. If you're configuring the round yourself rather than using a preset, give the input window enough time for participants to actually submit, and the compute window enough time for your worst observed program runtime.
  • Refunds don't expire, but they also don't auto-claim. Set a reminder to come back, or use a dApp that claims for you.

5. Choosing a secure committee

If you're configuring an E3 yourself (most users won't be — applications usually pick sensible defaults), the two parameters that matter are the committee size n and the decryption threshold m.

The high-level intuition:

  • n is how many operators are randomly selected to safeguard your round.
  • m is how many of those n operators must cooperate to decrypt the result.

Picking n (committee size)

n controls both availability and collusion resistance. A larger committee survives more simultaneous outages and is harder to bribe, but every additional operator adds coordination work to every other operator, so fees grow roughly quadratically in n. The right choice is a function of how much value is at stake in the round and how deep the live operator set is at the time.

General guidelines:

  • Keep n comfortably below the number of currently registered operators, so sortition has meaningful entropy.
  • Use a small n for development and rehearsal rounds where cost matters more than security.
  • Use a larger n for production rounds where the value being protected justifies the extra fee and longer key-generation time.
  • Going beyond a few dozen operators rarely buys meaningful additional security and primarily inflates the fee.

Picking m (decryption threshold)

m is the dial between safety (no-one can decrypt your data without m cooperating operators) and liveness (the round can only finish if at least m are online).

General guidelines:

  • A bare majority is the absolute floor: anything less than m ≥ ⌈n / 2⌉ + 1 means a minority could collude to decrypt your round on its own.
  • A two-thirds threshold (m ≈ ⌈2n / 3⌉) tracks the standard byzantine-fault-tolerant trust assumption and is a sensible middle ground.
  • Tighter thresholds (closer to n) increase safety against collusion but make the round more fragile — if too few operators are online when decryption time arrives, the round will time out.
  • Never set m == n. A single offline operator will then end the round with no decryption.

To make the random committee draw less variable, increase n rather than push the m / n ratio higher — doubling n roughly halves the chance the random sample is unusually unlucky.

6. At-a-glance checklist

Before you click "submit":

  • You've read the dApp's fee estimate and approved exactly that amount.
  • The committee size and threshold are appropriate for the value of the round.
  • The input window leaves enough time for everyone (including you) to submit.
  • You have a way to receive a notification when the round completes or fails.
  • You know which address to claim a refund from if the round fails.

After the round ends:

  • If it succeeded, you can read the result on-chain or in the dApp.
  • If it failed, wait for the RefundDistributionCalculated event, then claim from the Refund Manager using the same address that submitted the request.
